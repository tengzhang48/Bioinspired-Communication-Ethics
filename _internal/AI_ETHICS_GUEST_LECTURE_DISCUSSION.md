# Discussion Summary: J. Eric Mathis Guest Lecture Proposal

**Date:** May 14, 2026  
**Context:** Review of Dr. J. Eric Mathis's *Proposed Guest Lecturer Partnership Framework* (PDF uploaded to `_internal/`) in preparation for faculty discussion.  
**Course:** Bioinspired Communication & Ethics (EMIRGE-Bio NRT)

---

The document is **not a simple guest-lecture request**. It is a 9-page partnership framework proposing:

| Component | Scope |
|---|---|

**Key insight:** The internal materials (`REVIEWER_MEETING_TALKING_POINTS.md`, `REVIEWER_RESPONSE_MEMO.md`) anticipated a single Zoom session in Module 4. This proposal is 10–50× larger in scope and sits at the **NRT program level**, not the course level.

## 2. Core Concepts Explained

### "AI as Augmented Intelligence"
- **What it means:** AI reframed as a tool embedded within ecological/biological/human systems, not as autonomous computation.
- **Origin:** Popularized by IBM (c. 2016) to distinguish assistive AI from replacement fears.
- **Assessment:** A deliberately narrow, strategic framing. It assumes human control and cooperative relationships — which does not match what AI actually does in research (substitutes for writing labor, automates judgment, simulates expertise, fabricates citations).
- **Verdict:** Useful as a rhetorical example; inadequate as a primary framework for research ethics.

### "Formative Immergence" / Quantum Biology
- **What it means:** Mathis's own theoretical term extending emergence theory by claiming living systems have "quantum-informed dimensions of coherence" (citing microtubule superradiance research) that cannot be computationally replicated.
- **Assessment:** Philosophically specialized and scientifically contested. The leap from "biology may have quantum properties" to "therefore AI ethics must be ecological" is a philosophical preference, not a scientific deduction.
- **Verdict:** Too abstract for graduate students facing concrete research-integrity decisions. Risks obscuring practical problems behind systems-theory language.

---

## 3. Critical Questions Raised

### Should AI ethics be grounded in ecology and biology?
**Valuable contribution:** AI ethics has often been too anthropocentric, and ecology brings important dimensions that are easy to overlook — environmental costs of model training (energy, water, e-waste), AI applications in biodiversity monitoring, and agricultural decision-making. These are genuine ethical concerns that deserve more attention.

**Complementary rather than primary:** For a graduate research ethics course, the ecological framing works best as a **valuable complement** to other approaches rather than a replacement for them. Students also need practical tools for the professional decisions they face daily: disclosure, authorship, verification of AI-generated content, and navigating institutional policies. Rules and compliance are not sufficient on their own, but they are the necessary infrastructure through which journals, funders, and universities operate.

### Why connect AI with ecology?
Mathis approaches AI ethics from ecology and sustainability because that is his **disciplinary home and research expertise**. This is a legitimate and productive lens — scholars naturally bring their deepest training to ethical questions. At the same time, it is one lens among many. For students in this course (biology, engineering, physics, chemistry), the most immediate AI ethics questions tend to be professional and institutional: How do I disclose AI use in a manuscript? Who is accountable for a hallucinated citation? How do I maintain integrity when AI can draft faster than I can think?

The ecology-AI connection enriches the conversation — for example, by surfacing the environmental footprint of large language models — without needing to become the foundational frame for the entire course.

## 4. Pedagogical Resolution

**Best use of Mathis's material:** Treat his framework as a **case study in AI ethics discourse**, not as a framework to adopt.

### Rationale
Mathis's proposal is a vivid example of how scholars with different disciplinary backgrounds construct AI ethics differently. It demonstrates:
- How terminology is chosen strategically ("augmented intelligence" as rebranding)
- How home disciplines get imported into ethics arguments (ecology → AI)
- How scientific frontier claims lend authority to ethical positions (quantum biology)
- How guest-lecture proposals can simultaneously offer expertise AND advance personal research projects

### Proposed Activity: Mapping the AI Ethics Landscape
**Module:** Module 4, Session 5 — Artificial Intelligence in Research  
**Format:** 15–20 minute team analysis activity

Present three excerpts:
1. **Compliance Approach** (syllabus AI policy / Gerhard on detection)
2. **Professional Responsibility Approach** (ICMJE authorship / Lin on bias)
3. **Ecological/Systems Approach** (Mathis's "living systems" framing)

**Discussion prompts:**
- What does each framework **foreground**? What does it **obscure**?
- Which framework helps most with a disclosure decision? Which helps least with a ghostwriting accusation?
- Why might an ecologist find Mathis intuitive? Why might a computer scientist find it frustrating?
- Is "augmented intelligence" a **description** of AI or a **prescription** for how we should think about it?

**Pedagogical payoff:** Students learn AI ethics is contested space where disciplinary backgrounds, institutional interests, and rhetorical strategies shape what counts as an ethical problem.

## 5. Practical Recommendations

### For the Faculty Discussion with Mathis
- **Separate course content from program partnership.** The Module 4 guest lecture is an easy yes. The eLab, RCI, and microcredentials need NRT program-level approval.
- **Negotiate lecture scope.** Will Session 5 cover governance-aware AI and participatory evaluation (grounded and useful)? Or quantum biology and formative immergence (esoteric and tangential)?
- **Clarify deliverables.** Who develops slides and case studies? Does his lecture replace existing pre-readings or supplement them?
- **Be cautious about open-ended commitments.** "Throughout the NSF grant period" sounds expansive. Define actual time commitment per semester.

### Boundary-Setting Language
> "Your governance-aware AI and participatory evaluation frameworks are a strong fit for our Module 4 AI ethics session. However, the Story of the Plate platform, the eLab, and the ecological monitoring components are outside the scope of this course. Our students need research ethics training for proposal writing, authorship, and peer review — not garden-based experiential learning. Let's focus the guest lecture on the regulatory and governance dimensions of AI in research."

### For Course Materials
- Do **not** restructure existing modules around Mathis's framework.
- Do **consider** adding an ecological-AI topic to the Technology & Responsibility Report as an optional case study.
- Do **use** Mathis's framework as an object of analysis — it extends existing course tools (Alberts' systemic analysis, Resnik's principles, Module 2's framing lessons) by providing a concrete example of disciplinary bias in ethics argumentation.

## 7. Bottom Line

**Mathis's proposal is intellectually serious but organizationally ambitious.** The course-level response should be bounded and pedagogical: invite the guest lecture, use the framework as a case study in ethics discourse, and redirect program-scale proposals (eLab, RCI, microcredentials) to the NRT leadership level.

The strongest negotiating position:

> *"We can use you in Module 4 immediately. The governance-aware AI angle enriches what we already teach. But the eLab, Story of the Plate, and RCI partnership are program-level conversations — they belong with the NRT leadership, not on a single course site."*
