Writing Principles and Their Boundaries

Two well-known lab guides to writing papers, from the Whitesides group and the Weitz lab, agree on a lot. On a few basic points they give opposite advice. Seeing where and why they differ helps you choose what fits your field and your journal.

How to read this deck: every quotation names its source. When the two guides disagree, the deck shows both sides. Use ↓ and ↑ (or J and K) to move between slides.

Whose advice is this?

Before you follow or repeat a writing rule, ask three questions: Who says it? In which document? For what kind of paper? This deck uses two sources and labels each piece of advice with its source.

Whitesides group (2004)

George M. Whitesides, “Whitesides’ Group: Writing a Paper,” Advanced Materials.1

Written for: his chemistry group’s papers, in the style of the American Chemical Society (ACS). The text is based on a group handout from October 4, 1989.

Weitz lab (revised 2012)

Harvard Weitz lab, Guide to Good Paper Writing, revised October 4, 2012.2

Written for: short papers (letters) in journals such as Science, Nature, and Physical Review Letters (PRL).

What a lab guide is

Each guide describes how one research group writes, for its own students, in one field, at one time. Neither one is a journal requirement or a rule for every field. A piece of advice is only as strong as its source.

2 / 8

Hypotheses, data, conclusions — Whitesides

Whitesides calls a paper “an organized description of hypotheses, data and conclusions.”1 His outline is built from these three parts.

Hypotheses

What you propose and then test. A goal such as “make a tougher gel” is not yet a hypothesis. A hypothesis says what you expect, and why.

Data

The figures, tables, and schemes that test each hypothesis. In Whitesides’ outline they come first; full sentences come later.

Conclusions

What the data show about each hypothesis, and what is still open.

Try it with your outline

Write your team’s central claim in one sentence. Under it, write the hypothesis it rests on and the one figure that would test it. If you cannot name that figure yet, you have found the experiment or analysis that is still missing.

3 / 8

Start the outline early — Whitesides

“A paper is not just an archival device for storing a completed research program; it is also a structure for planning your research in progress.” Whitesides 20041
“An outline is a written plan of the organization of a paper, including the data on which it rests.” Whitesides 20041

His point is practical: when you outline early, you can see which experiments are still missing. That is why he tells his group to “start writing possible outlines for papers early in a project. Do not wait until the ‘end’.”

Order — Whitesides

“Organize in order of importance, not in chronological order.” The order in which you did the work is rarely the order in which a reader needs it.

The one-sentence summary — Weitz

Before drafting, write what the paper says in “one sentence, two at most.” Weitz adds that you should “never be afraid to change these sentences… this will invariably lead to a better paper.”

4 / 8

Where the guides disagree: tense

Both guides answer the same question: which tense should you use for your results? They give opposite answers.

Whitesides — past tense

“Describe experimental results uniformly in the past tense.”

Not: Addition of water gives product.
But: Addition of water gave product.

Weitz — present tense

“Generally use the present tense… You can also use the past tense for things that you did in the past… It is simpler to use the present tense for everything.”

Discuss
  1. What could explain the difference: the field, the type of paper, or the time when each guide was written?
  2. Whitesides wrote for ACS chemistry papers; Weitz wrote for short letters in Science, Nature, and PRL. Does that explain it?
  3. Open three recent papers in your field. Which tense do they use for results? Count first, then decide.
What to do

When good sources disagree, check your target journal and recent papers in your field. Then choose one style and use it consistently in your own paper.

5 / 8

The abstract comes last: here the guides agree

Whitesides

“Abstract — Do not write an abstract. That can be done when the paper is complete.”

Weitz

“The abstract should be written last, after the rest of the paper is complete.”

A rough shape for an abstract — Weitz

“It should consist roughly, not literally, of: 2 sentences from the Introduction / 2 sentences from the Conclusion / 1 sentence about the importance of the paper.” Weitz lab guide, rev. 10/4/20122
“Roughly, not literally”

The 2-2-1 shape is a starting point, not a formula. The guide says so in the same sentence.

On the introduction — Weitz

“It is often also the main paragraph that determines whether or not a paper is accepted.” The word “often” matters: the introduction is important, but it is not the only thing reviewers judge.

For our Writing Portfolio

Our portfolio is a short manuscript. Follow both guides: draft the outline first, then the sections, and write the abstract last, once the rest is written.

6 / 8

Figures: in the text or in the caption?

“Discuss the figures in the text… The reader should not have to stop and read the caption to understand what is in the figure and why it is important. Note that this is not a universal rule: both Science and Nature prefer to have a very detailed caption, with very little discussion of the figure in the text.” Weitz lab guide, rev. 10/4/20122

Many summaries of this guide quote only the first sentence. The second sentence matters: Science and Nature prefer the opposite.

Point first (the style Weitz prefers)

“The interface toughens as the network is anchored covalently, as shown in Fig. 3.”

The reader learns the point first; the figure is the evidence.

Location first

“In Fig. 3, we show the interfacial toughness as a function of anchoring density.”

The reader learns where to look before learning why.

The two example sentences were written for this course; they are not quotations.

Check your journal

Where you discuss a figure depends on the journal. Read the target journal’s author instructions, then follow them.

7 / 8

Guidelines, not laws

“Please remember that, like all cases with the English language, these are not rules, but are guidelines, and there will certainly be times that they do not apply or that they should be changed.” Weitz lab guide, opening paragraph2

This sentence opens the Weitz guide, but it is often left out when the list is shared. Without it, the guidelines start to sound like strict rules.

Left: how the advice is often repeated. Right: what the source actually says.
Often repeated asWhat the source says
“Never begin a sentence with This.” Whitesides: “The word ‘this’ must always be followed by a noun, so that its reference is explicit.” His own correct examples begin with it: “This reaction is fast”; “This observation leads us to conclude.” Weitz: a problem only if you do it “frequently.”
“Never name living people.” Weitz: “The only personal names you should include in the text of a paper (not counting article references or citations) are names of people who are dead.” The part in parentheses matters: citations are not affected.
“No parentheses. No i.e. Forbidden phrases.” These are in the Weitz list, under its own “not rules, but guidelines” opening. They are style choices for short letters, not bans.
“Don’t be historical.” Weitz: “don’t be historical, be as clear as possible.” The point is clarity. It does not forbid explaining how the work developed.
Writing is not everything

Clear writing helps readers and reviewers, but it does not decide acceptance by itself. Reviews also depend on whether the work is new and correct, whether it fits the journal, and which reviewers read it.

Test a rule yourself

Pick one rule that you can count. For example: the tense used for results, the number of parentheses per sentence, or whether the discussion starts with the main finding. What to write

Not “the rule is right” or “the rule is wrong.” Write the rule again in one sentence that says where it applies: which field, which type of paper, which journal.

What three examples can show

Whether the rule holds in these three examples. They cannot show what is normal for a whole field, and a finished paper cannot show how its authors drafted it.

Exit ticket

One rule, with where it applies, and one claim your outline cannot support yet.
