# Reviewer-Response Memo: AI Ethics Integration in Bioinspired Communication and Ethics

## Purpose

This memo summarizes how the current course, *Bioinspired Communication and Ethics*, already addresses AI-related ethics content and identifies the most appropriate way to respond to the external reviewers' recommendations. The central conclusion is that AI ethics is already meaningfully embedded in the course. The next step is not to create AI ethics content from scratch, but to formalize, label, and assess an existing cross-course AI ethics strand.

## Executive Summary

The reviewers' recommendations are directionally helpful, especially in their emphasis on transparency, responsible AI use, peer review, and assessment. However, they understate the extent to which the current course already addresses these issues. AI is already integrated across the syllabus and across multiple modules through activities on disclosure, hallucinations, authorship, peer review, reliability, and ethical reasoning.

For that reason, the strongest response is not to describe the course as lacking AI ethics content. Instead, the response should emphasize that the course already contains a distributed AI ethics framework and that future revisions will make this framework more explicit through clearer learning outcomes, disclosure guidance, rubrics, and assessment language.

The reviewers' longer section on "Ethical Considerations for AI in Synthetic Biology" should be treated cautiously. It raises potentially interesting ideas for advanced discussion, but it is too narrow and too philosophically specialized to serve as the main organizing framework for a graduate course intended for a broad interdisciplinary audience. AI ethics and synthetic biology ethics are each major domains. Synthetic biology is better positioned as one possible case-study area within a broader AI ethics structure, not as the defining lens for the entire course.

## Current Strengths of the Course

The current course already includes substantial AI-related content in several places.

At the syllabus level, the course includes learning outcomes related to AI use and science communication ethics, and it already contains a practical AI use policy. That policy requires disclosure of AI-generated content, prohibits the use of AI-generated citations and references, and frames AI literacy as a matter of critical judgment rather than simple prohibition.

Within the proposal-writing module, students examine AI's role in the research enterprise, including AI hype cycles, hypothesis generation, AI-assisted review, and the limits of AI evaluation. This material already invites students to consider how AI affects funding culture, review practices, and scientific judgment.

Within the scientific writing module, students compare AI-generated feedback with human peer review, assess hallucinations and attribution failures, and reflect on what kinds of feedback AI can and cannot reliably provide. This is already a strong applied ethics exercise because it moves beyond abstract policy discussion and asks students to evaluate AI use in their own writing practice.

Within the research ethics module, students directly engage with AI ghostwriting, bias, authorship, disclosure, accountability, detection failure, regulatory frameworks, and the limits of current oversight models. This is already robust AI ethics content by any reasonable standard.

The pedagogy also already aligns well with the reviewers' suggestions. The course uses team-based learning, structured discussion, debates, mock panels, peer review, and case-based reasoning throughout. In other words, the course does not need a new teaching philosophy to deliver AI ethics; it already has an effective instructional model in place.

## How the Reviewers' Recommendations Map to Existing Course Materials

Several reviewer recommendations are already substantially addressed in the current course.

The recommendation to integrate AI ethics into the course has already been implemented in distributed form. AI-related content is present in the syllabus and in the proposal, writing, and ethics modules.

The recommendation to teach transparent AI use and reporting is partially implemented. The syllabus already requires disclosure and warns against fabricated references. What remains missing is a more standardized disclosure format that students can use consistently across assignments.

The recommendation to practice ethical peer review of AI-related work is also already substantially present. Students compare AI and human feedback, analyze AI evaluation limits, and discuss the ethical boundaries of AI-assisted reviewing. What remains missing is a formal rubric that explicitly addresses AI-assisted work.

The recommendation to use team-based learning for AI ethics case studies is already well aligned with the course design. This is a strength the response should highlight.

The recommendation to address authorship, intellectual property, and data responsibility is likewise already present, especially in the ethics module. The main opportunity here is to make these pieces more visibly connected to dissertation proposals, manuscripts, and professional practice.

## What Is Still Missing

The main gaps are not in basic content coverage, but in organization, assessment, and signaling.

First, the course does not yet present its AI content as a single explicit strand running across modules. AI appears in several strong places, but the structure is implicit rather than formally named.

Second, the course would benefit from a set of AI-specific learning outcomes that are stated more explicitly. The current materials already address topics such as disclosure, bias, authorship, and reliability, but these ideas could be framed more directly as course-level competencies.

Third, the course would benefit from a standard AI disclosure statement or reporting template for use in proposals, manuscripts, presentations, and team reports.

Fourth, the course would benefit from rubric language that evaluates ethical AI use, transparent reporting, and the quality of students' reasoning about AI limitations.

Finally, the reviewers' recommendations about microcredentials, mentor training, student-led seminars, and evaluation logic models may be valuable at the NRT program level, but they are not things that can be demonstrated or verified through this course repository alone. Those items should be discussed as program-level opportunities rather than as missing pieces of this course site.

An especially practical near-term step would be to refine the AI distribution across modules in a way that matches the course's existing logic. A brief AI discussion in Module 1 would help establish expectations around collaboration, responsible tool use, and disclosure early in the semester. Then, in Module 4, the reviewer or another scholar working actively on AI in research ethics could be invited to give a guest lecture by Zoom for the dedicated "Artificial Intelligence in Research" session. This would allow the course to benefit directly from current expertise without forcing an immediate large-scale redesign of the course structure.

## Recommended Framing for the Reviewer Response

The response should acknowledge the value of the reviewers' recommendations while making clear that the course already contains a strong foundation in AI ethics.

A useful framing would be:

"We appreciate the reviewers' emphasis on responsible and transparent AI use in research training. Our current course, *Bioinspired Communication and Ethics*, already integrates AI-related ethics across proposal writing, scientific writing, and research ethics, including attention to disclosure, hallucinations, peer review, authorship, and responsible use. In response to the reviewers' comments, we will make this existing AI ethics strand more explicit by refining learning outcomes, adding structured disclosure guidance, and strengthening assessment language around ethical AI practice."

That framing is accurate, constructive, and responsive without suggesting that the course currently lacks substantive AI content.

## Recommended Direction for Future Revision

The most effective way to strengthen the course is to organize AI-related material under a broader interdisciplinary ethics framework rather than around a narrow subfield.

The course could be revised around four cross-cutting areas:

1. Transparent AI use and disclosure in research and communication.
2. Reliability, hallucinations, bias, and the limits of AI judgment.
3. Authorship, peer review, accountability, and intellectual responsibility.
4. Domain-specific case studies, with synthetic biology as one possible application rather than the sole framing device.

This structure would preserve the breadth of the course, align with the reviewers' goals, and avoid overcommitting the course to one domain-specific philosophical framework.

Within that structure, the AI strand could begin earlier in Module 1 with a short discussion of AI use in teamwork, shared expectations, and disclosure norms. That change would make AI part of the course's team-science foundation rather than something introduced only after students have already begun collaborative work.

The dedicated AI session in Module 4 would then remain the natural place for a guest lecture. A Zoom session focused on AI ghostwriting, hallucinations, detection, and regulatory frameworks would fit especially well there because those topics already align with the session's stated scope. This would signal responsiveness to reviewer expertise while preserving the course's overall structure.

## Note on AI and Synthetic Biology

The reviewers' synthetic biology discussion may still be useful, but it should be repositioned.

Rather than treating "AI in synthetic biology" as the central ethics frame, it would be better used as an advanced case study within a broader discussion of AI in scientific research. This approach is better suited to the course's interdisciplinary audience and better reflects the fact that AI ethics and synthetic biology ethics are both broad areas with distinct but overlapping concerns.

In short, synthetic biology can be an example. It should not be the umbrella.

## Conclusion

The course is already doing meaningful work in AI ethics. The strongest response to the reviewers is therefore not to promise an entirely new AI ethics curriculum, but to show that a strong foundation already exists and that the next phase will focus on clearer articulation, stronger assessment, and better integration across existing course components.

This response is both accurate to the current materials and aligned with the reviewers' broader goals. It also leaves room for a constructive next step: introducing AI earlier in the team-building module and inviting a reviewer with relevant expertise to contribute to the dedicated AI session in Module 4 while the broader AI ethics strand is being further formalized.
