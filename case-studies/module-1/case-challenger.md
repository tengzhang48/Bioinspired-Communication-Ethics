# Evidence Packet: Challenger and the Night-Before Teleconference

***STS-51-L, January 27–28, 1986 | Technical dissent, contractor advice, management authority, and failed escalation in a safety-critical system***

---

## Memorial and Learning Frame

On January 28, 1986, *Challenger* and its crew were lost 73 seconds after liftoff. The seven crew members were Francis R. Scobee, Michael J. Smith, Ronald E. McNair, Ellison S. Onizuka, Judith A. Resnik, Gregory B. Jarvis, and S. Christa McAuliffe. NASA commemorates them through its Day of Remembrance; their families established the Challenger Center as an educational legacy.

We study the launch-decision record to improve how teams handle uncertain evidence, dissent, authority, and escalation—not to turn a tragedy into a simple morality tale. Begin with the [NASA STS-51-L memorial page](https://www.nasa.gov/mission/sts-51l/) and [NASA's remembrance history](https://www.nasa.gov/history/35-years-ago-remembering-challenger-and-her-crew/).

---

## How to Read This Packet

The principal source is [Chapter V of the Rogers Commission report](https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1ch5.htm), especially its chronology and findings. It combines documents, a Commission reconstruction, and testimony given after the accident. These are not identical kinds of evidence:

- **Documented event:** supported by a contemporaneous chart, fax, schedule, or multiple-source chronology.
- **Attributed testimony:** a participant's later recollection; important evidence, but not an omniscient transcript.
- **Commission finding:** the investigating Commission's conclusion after reviewing the record.
- **Interpretation:** a later explanatory label or causal claim that must be argued rather than treated as a recorded fact.

## Evidence-Based Sequence

| Approximate time (EST) | What the record supports | Evidence status and limit |
|---|---|---|
| 5:45 p.m., January 27 | Morton Thiokol personnel raised low-temperature concerns and, in the Commission's summary, expressed an opinion that launch should be delayed. Another conference with data and more participants was arranged. | Commission chronology, not a verbatim transcript. Later testimony differed on whether participants understood the first call as a delay recommendation. |
| 8:45–10:30 p.m. | During the second teleconference, Thiokol presented O-ring erosion, blow-by, test, and prior-flight data. Robert Lund presented the initial recommendation not to launch below 53°F, the lowest temperature in the relevant flight experience. NASA participants challenged the recommendation and its data basis. | Documents and testimony synthesized by the Commission. Participants differed in how they characterized the tone and pressure. |
| About 10:30 p.m. | Joseph Kilminster requested roughly five minutes off the communication loop for a Thiokol caucus. The Commission chronology says the caucus lasted about 30 minutes; George Hardy's submitted statement says **30–35 minutes**. | The requested duration and actual duration are different facts. “A five-minute decision” is inaccurate. See [Hardy's statement in Volume V](https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v5p887.htm). |
| During the caucus | Roger Boisjoly and Arnold Thompson continued to oppose launch. A final review involved Thiokol managers Jerald Mason, Robert Lund, Joseph Kilminster, and Calvin Wiggins. Boisjoly testified that Mason told Lund to “take off his engineering hat and put on his management hat.” | The participants and management review appear in the Commission reconstruction. The quoted words are Boisjoly's attributed recollection, not an audio recording. |
| About 11:00–11:45 p.m. | Thiokol returned with a recommendation to launch. Kilminster read the rationale and later signed and faxed the written recommendation. Allan McDonald continued to argue for delay at Kennedy. | Documented recommendation plus attributed testimony. Reversing the recommendation did not mean that all technical dissent had disappeared. |
| Overnight and morning of January 28 | The Commission found that the initial recommendation, continuing engineer objections, and relevant O-ring history did not reach key Level I/II officials or the Launch Director. *Challenger* launched at 11:38 a.m. | Commission finding and NASA mission record. The Commission concluded that the launch-decision process was seriously flawed; that finding is broader than assigning the entire failure to one conversation or person. |

## Decision-Authority Map

This map distinguishes technical voice, contractor sign-off, program authority, and escalation. It does not claim that every contractual or launch-control rule is captured here.

| Actor or level | Documented role in this episode | Authority or information limit |
|---|---|---|
| Thiokol engineers, including Boisjoly and Thompson | Developed and explained technical concerns; continued to oppose launch during the caucus. | Advisory voice; they were not members of the final four-person management review and did not sign the final recommendation. |
| Lund and Thiokol senior management | Lund presented the initial engineering recommendation; Mason, Lund, Kilminster, and Wiggins conducted the final management review. | They determined the contractor's final institutional recommendation. The record should not be simplified into a formal individual vote by everyone present. |
| Kilminster, Thiokol program vice president | Communicated the recommendation and signed the fax recommending launch. | His signature documented Thiokol's position; it did not document unanimous technical agreement. |
| NASA Level III project personnel, including Mulloy and Reinartz | Questioned the initial recommendation and received Thiokol's revised recommendation. Reinartz testified that he decided not to elevate the issue to Level II. | They controlled an important escalation path but were not the entire launch-authority structure. |
| NASA Level II/I officials and Launch Director | Occupied the higher program and launch-decision structure that should receive critical concerns. | Arnold Aldrich, Jesse Moore, and Gene Thomas testified that they did not know of Thiokol's objections before launch. Authority without the material dissent was not an informed redundant check. |

The cited record does **not** clearly establish a formal contractor “veto.” Several NASA participants testified that they would not recommend launch against a contractor no-launch recommendation, but operational reliance is not necessarily the same as a formally specified veto. That distinction is a question for further documentary research, not an assumption for class discussion.

## What Is Documented—and What Remains Interpretation

**Documented or found by the Commission:** an initial no-launch-below-53°F recommendation; NASA challenges; a requested five-minute caucus lasting approximately 30–35 minutes; continuing objections by engineers; a management review; a signed recommendation to launch; and failure to communicate crucial dissent and O-ring history to higher decision authorities.

**Attributed and therefore kept attributed:** McDonald and Boisjoly testified that Hardy said he was “appalled” by the initial recommendation; Boisjoly recalled the management-hat statement and described perceived launch pressure; other participants characterized the exchange differently.

**Not established merely by this record:** each participant's private motive; that schedule or publicity pressure caused a particular person to reverse position; that everyone agreed after the caucus; or that a single label such as “groupthink,” “bad communication,” or “five-minute decision” fully explains the launch decision. Such claims may be hypotheses, but they require additional evidence and should identify whose testimony supports or disputes them.

## Outcome-Bias Counterfactual

Imagine the same pre-launch process followed by a flight with no visible O-ring failure. The result would remove the tragedy from that mission, but it would not retroactively supply missing evidence, communicate unresolved dissent, or create an escalation record. Conversely, knowing the actual loss does not prove that every disagreement or management choice was unreasonable for the same reason.

Judge the process using what was available **before** launch:

- Was the burden of evidence appropriate for a safety-critical uncertainty?
- Was the change in recommendation traceable, and did it address the original concern?
- Did the signed position accurately preserve material dissent?
- Did the people with launch authority receive the decision-relevant information?

## Transfer to a Student Team Charter—and Its Limits

Useful transfers include naming who may pause submission, recording unresolved high-consequence concerns, separating advice from sign-off, requiring a reasoned response before closure, preserving minority views, and defining an escalation route outside the immediate team.

The analogy has strict limits:

- A course project is not a human-safety flight system; do not equate ordinary deadline stress with the loss of life.
- A student Charter cannot replace engineering standards, independent safety organizations, contracts, regulation, or institutional reporting channels.
- Technical validity is not settled by majority vote. A Charter should identify who verifies evidence and when external expertise is required.
- A signature records review and the current decision; it must not erase dissent or waive anyone's right to escalate a safety, integrity, or ethical concern.
- If the team's own authority is insufficient, the Charter must point to the instructor, laboratory leadership, safety office, research-integrity office, or another appropriate external authority.

## Discussion Questions

1. Select three statements in the sequence. Which are documented events, attributed testimony, Commission findings, or interpretations? What changes when those categories are blurred?
2. Trace the concern from engineer to contractor management to NASA Level III and then toward Levels II/I. Where could someone advise, sign, pause, decide, or escalate? Which role was ambiguous or ineffective?
3. What should a signed recommendation contain when technical dissent remains? Who may close the concern, and what evidence should be required?
4. Apply the no-accident counterfactual. Which judgments about the process remain unchanged, and which judgments were influenced by knowing the outcome?
5. Draft one Charter clause for unresolved high-consequence concerns. Then identify what that clause cannot accomplish without an external standard or authority.
6. Why does describing the caucus as a “five-minute decision” distort the record? Does correcting it to approximately 30–35 minutes change the central accountability questions?

### Source Caution

The Rogers Commission is the authoritative primary investigation used here, but its narrative includes retrospective and sometimes conflicting participant testimony. The cited record does not provide a complete audio transcript of the teleconference. For that reason, this packet attributes recollections, avoids claims about private motives, and treats the Commission's institutional findings separately from any one witness's account.
