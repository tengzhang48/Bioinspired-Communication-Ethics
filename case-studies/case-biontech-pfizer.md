# Case Study: BioNTech and Pfizer — Project Lightspeed

**BioNTech (Mainz, Germany) and Pfizer (New York, US), January 2020 – December 2020 | A 1,300-person company, a 70,000-person company, eleven months, and the first authorized mRNA vaccine.**

---

## Setting

In early January 2020, Uğur Şahin, the co-founder and CEO of BioNTech, read about a cluster of pneumonia cases in Wuhan, China. BioNTech had been working on mRNA technology since 2008, primarily for cancer immunotherapy applications, and had built a research platform that could in principle be redirected at a viral target. Şahin and his co-founder and wife Özlem Türeci spent the weekend of January 24–26 working through what such a redirection would require.

On Monday, January 27, Şahin presented the case to BioNTech's executive team. The proposal — code-named Project Lightspeed — was to redirect a substantial fraction of the company's resources toward a COVID-19 mRNA vaccine, despite no confirmed pandemic, no commercial agreement with any partner, no manufacturing capacity at scale, and no precedent for authorization of an mRNA vaccine in any human disease. BioNTech had never produced a marketed product. The company was carrying approximately €400 million in debt at the start of 2020.

The executive team approved.

## The Setup

By March 2020, BioNTech had identified twenty candidate mRNA constructs and was running parallel preclinical evaluations. The technical work was on track. The structural problem was different: BioNTech could not, on its own, scale manufacturing to billions of doses, run a global clinical trial, navigate regulatory pathways across dozens of jurisdictions, or distribute a vaccine that required ultra-cold-chain storage. The company needed a partner of fundamentally different scale.

In mid-March 2020, Şahin contacted Kathrin Jansen, head of vaccine R&D at Pfizer. The two companies had previously collaborated on an influenza vaccine project that was still in early development. Şahin proposed expanding that collaboration to cover COVID-19. Jansen took the proposal to Pfizer's CEO Albert Bourla. Pfizer agreed within days.

The formal collaboration agreement, however, took longer to draft. Throughout March and into April 2020, BioNTech and Pfizer were operating without a finalized contract. The legal agreement covering financial terms, intellectual property division, and decision rights for the joint program was being negotiated by lawyers while the scientific teams were already exchanging data.

## The Trust Decisions

Three specific BioNTech decisions during this period are what make Project Lightspeed unusual as a teamwork case.

**The "share everything" instruction.** Şahin instructed his team to share preliminary data, scientific approaches, and manufacturing details with their Pfizer counterparts *before* the legal agreement was finalized. The standard practice in biotech partnerships is to limit information exchange until contracts are signed and intellectual property is protected. Şahin's reasoning, which he has since stated publicly, was that the alternative — waiting for legal clarity — would cost weeks the pandemic did not allow. The cost of being wrong was roughly the company's existence.

**The parallel-not-sequential clinical trial.** Vaccine clinical trials normally proceed in three sequential phases, with each phase analyzed before the next begins. Project Lightspeed compressed this by running phases in overlapping rather than sequential windows. The first dose to a human was administered in late April 2020; Phase 2/3 began in late July; manufacturing scale-up began before Phase 3 efficacy data was available. Each of these decisions required BioNTech to commit resources before earlier phases had returned definitive results. If any phase failed, the resources committed to later phases would be lost.

**The manufacturing scale-up.** By August 2020, BioNTech and Pfizer were manufacturing vaccine doses at commercial scale despite no efficacy data and no authorization. The decision was made jointly but was financially asymmetric. Pfizer absorbed most of the manufacturing cost (and could absorb the loss if the vaccine failed); BioNTech contributed its mRNA platform expertise and accepted that any failure would be existential for the smaller company. The two organizations' risk exposures were not equal, but the decision proceeded.

## The Critical Moment

On Sunday, November 8, 2020, the independent Data Monitoring Committee for the BNT162b2 Phase 3 trial reviewed the first interim efficacy analysis. The result, communicated to the BioNTech and Pfizer leadership early Sunday afternoon European time, showed efficacy above 90 percent — substantially higher than the 50–70 percent the team had been quietly hoping for.

The teams now had less than 24 hours to decide what to do with the information before public announcement on Monday morning. Multiple decisions had to be made jointly between BioNTech and Pfizer in those hours: how to communicate the result, when to file for emergency use authorization, what to say to regulators in twenty-plus jurisdictions simultaneously, and how to coordinate the logistics of shipping a product that did not yet have authorization in any country.

The teams reached agreement on the announcement, the regulatory filing strategy, and the initial distribution plan in under 18 hours.

The vaccine received FDA Emergency Use Authorization on December 11, 2020. The first non-trial dose was administered in the UK on December 8 and in the US on December 14. The mRNA platform itself — which had been an unproven academic technology in 2019 — became the basis for a category of medical product that did not exist before.

## Discussion Questions

1. **Şahin instructed his team to share data with Pfizer before the legal agreement was finalized.** Standard practice is the opposite. What conditions made this decision visionary rather than reckless? Could you have made it from his position? What information would you have needed to feel confident in the decision?

2. **Risk asymmetry.** Pfizer (70,000 employees, multiple marketed products) and BioNTech (1,300 employees, no marketed products, €400M in debt) were exposed to very different consequences if the vaccine failed. Most "trust between partners" framing treats the partners as symmetric. What does asymmetric risk exposure require of a partnership that symmetric risk does not?

3. **Parallel-not-sequential clinical trials.** The decision to run trial phases in overlapping windows committed resources before earlier phases had returned definitive results. This is the same structural decision a research team makes when deciding whether to write the paper before the analysis is complete, or to begin manuscript drafting before all experiments are run. What conditions make parallel-not-sequential work safer? What conditions make it dangerous?

4. **The 18-hour decision window on November 8.** When the efficacy result came in, the teams had to make multiple consequential decisions jointly under tight time pressure. What had to have been built in advance — in terms of trust, working norms, and decision authority — for those 18 hours to have been productive rather than chaotic? Compare this implicitly to the five-minute caucus that overrode the engineering recommendation in the Challenger case: both were decisions made under time pressure by small groups, but the outcomes were very different.

5. **Compare Project Lightspeed with Challenger.** Both were team decisions under extreme pressure with non-negotiable deadlines. One produced a vaccine; one produced a disaster. *What structural conditions* — not personality, not luck — *separated the two outcomes?* Identify two or three concrete elements present in one case and absent in the other.
